## The basics

- A reasonable accommodation is any change that lets a person with a disability do the essential functions of a job or have equal opportunity.
- Covered: employers with 15 or more employees, government employers, employment agencies, labor organizations.
- **Essential functions are never reassignable.** Marginal (non-essential) functions may be restructured or reassigned.
- **Undue hardship** means significant difficulty or expense. It is a high bar and is decided case by case.
- **Cost facts:** most accommodations cost nothing, and the median cost of a one-time accommodation is $300.

## The interactive process

A flexible, collaborative process with three steps:

1. Understand the need (what limitation is getting in the way).
2. Explore solutions (brainstorm accommodations with the employee).
3. Implement (put the chosen accommodation in place, then follow up).

JAN's A-to-Z documents also use a 9-step checklist that expands these three steps: identify limitations → gather information → brainstorm → explore resources → choose → implement → monitor → follow up → document.

## How to request an accommodation

- Use plain English. There is **no need to say "ADA" or "reasonable accommodation."**
- The request can be verbal or written. Written is recommended — it creates a record.
- Make the request to a supervisor, manager, or HR.
- The request can come from the person, a family member, or a representative.
- Do not wait until performance problems appear — ask before.
- Free MyJAN accounts let users save JAN resources for later.

## Medical documentation

- An employer may ask for documentation only when the disability or the need for accommodation is not known or obvious.
- The employer may require only what is needed to establish that the person has an ADA disability and that the disability requires the accommodation. They cannot demand more.
- The employer cannot ask for documentation when the need is obvious or when enough information has already been given.
- A provider's letter should state the disability, the limitation, and why the accommodation helps — nothing more.

## Confidentiality

- All medical information must be kept **separate from personnel files**, accessible only to authorized personnel.
- EEOC exceptions: supervisors are told only about restrictions and needed accommodations; first-aid/safety staff may be told if the condition might need emergency treatment; government officials investigating compliance; workers' compensation offices.
- Coworkers get nothing. A manager who is asked about someone else's accommodation may only say it is for legitimate business reasons or compliance with federal law.

## Accommodation categories

Facilities access; job restructuring; flexible, part-time, or modified scheduling; policy modifications (telework, attendance, dress code, break policies); assistive technology; changed tests or training methods; written instructions and visual aids; interpreters, captioners, and job coaches; reassignment to a vacant position; leave.

## Disability-specific accommodation ideas (all JAN-sourced)

### Autism

- Noise-canceling headsets, sound panels, white-noise machines
- Flexible or modified schedules
- Telework
- Job restructuring (moving non-essential tasks to others)
- Written instructions, task flow charts, checklists
- Job coaches
- Speech-generating or communication apps
- Real JAN cases: getting interview questions in advance; electronic instead of in-person team communication; pre-recording a presentation instead of giving it live

### ADHD

- For hyperactivity: structured breaks, job coach, work from home, adjusted supervision, EAP referral, private workspace
- For focus: quiet workspace, noise cancellation or white noise, uninterrupted work time
- For time management: mentor, to-do lists, timers, calendars, apps
- Real JAN cases: trial telework that became permanent; shifting the schedule 2 hours earlier; color-coded instructions

### Anxiety

- Flexible or modified schedules, telework
- Rest area or private space
- One-on-one communication instead of group meetings
- Support person or support animal
- Checklists, apps for anxiety and stress
- Job coach
- Reassignment out of the triggering role
- Real JAN cases: reassignment out of an anxiety-triggering unit; a job coach for workplace social communication

### Bladder impairments (including incontinence)

JAN's bladder-impairment page names functional incontinence, urge incontinence, and stress incontinence. Toileting/grooming accommodation ideas:

- Flexible and modified break schedules (including unscheduled breaks as needed)
- Telework
- Worksite redesign (for example, moving the workstation closer to a restroom)
- Keeping spare supplies or a change of clothes at work
- Access to a private or single-use restroom where available
- Real JAN case: an employee who experienced incontinence was having accidents at work. The employer allowed the employee to keep a change of clothes at the office and take additional restroom breaks.

### GI disorders / IBS

- Flexible break schedules; shift-end extension to make up restroom time
- Workstation moved closer to the restroom (JAN notes this cost virtually nothing in one case)
- Later arrival time
- Telework during flare-ups
- See: https://askjan.org/disabilities/Gastrointestinal-Disorders.cfm and https://askjan.org/articles/Addressing-Accommodation-Needs-Specific-to-Gastrointestinal-Disorders.cfm

### Note on bowel-control needs

JAN has no dedicated page for fecal incontinence. Map those needs to the GI Disorders pages and to the toileting/grooming limitation category. Say this openly — do not pretend a JAN page exists that does not.
