# Executive Functioning Deficits — Accommodation Solutions (JAN)

Source: https://askjan.org/articles/Executive-Functioning-Deficits.cfm (extracted 2026-10-06).
JAN materials are U.S. Government works. Verify against the live page before quoting —
pages are updated periodically.

## What it covers

Executive functions are high-level mental processes: planning, organizing, strategizing,
paying attention to and remembering details, starting and stopping actions, forming
concepts, thinking abstractly, and monitoring/regulating behavior.

JAN notes these deficits can appear with: depression, OCD, PTSD, schizophrenia, ADD/ADHD,
intellectual and learning disabilities, autism, and brain injuries.

**Why this page matters for the skill:** 13 JAN disability pages have no accommodation
list of their own (Fetal Alcohol Syndrome, Learning Disability, Lupus, Lyme Disease,
Marfan Syndrome, Anxiety Disorder, Bipolar Disorder, Depression, Eating Disorders, OCD,
Personality Disorder, Phobias, Schizophrenia) and point here instead. When the user's
condition is one of those, use the ideas below.

## Accommodation ideas by area

### Time management

Difficulty marking time as it passes, gauging how long tasks take, preparing for or
remembering future work activities.

- Divide large assignments into small tasks or chunks
- Set a timer to sound an alarm after ample time to complete a task
- Provide a checklist of assignments
- Plan and structure times of transition and shifts in activities
- Supply an electronic or handheld organizer, and train on using it effectively
- Use a wall calendar to emphasize due dates
- Develop a color-coded system (each color = a task, event, or level of importance)
- Allow a co-worker or supervisor to add calendar entries or double-check entries

### Memory

Difficulty completing tasks, remembering job duties, or recalling daily actions.

- Provide written instructions and checklists
- Allow use of a recorder
- Allow additional training time for new tasks
- Offer training refreshers
- Provide minutes of meetings and trainings
- Use a flow-chart to indicate steps in a task
- Provide verbal or pictorial cues
- Use a color-coding scheme to prioritize tasks
- Use notebooks, planners, or sticky notes to record information
- Use sticky notes as reminders of important dates or tasks
- Provide labels or bulletin board cues to help locate items

### Concentration

Decreased concentration from auditory distractions (office traffic, chatter, elevator
doors, common office noises) or visual distractions.

- Reduce auditory distractions: noise-canceling headset; sound absorption panels;
  white-noise machine; relocate or redesign the workspace away from audible distractions
- Reduce visual distractions: space enclosures (cubicle walls); reduce clutter;
  relocate or redesign the workspace away from visual distractions
- Breaks for mental fatigue: short walks, getting up for water, rotating through
  varied tasks
- Job restructuring so the hardest tasks happen when the employee has the most
  mental energy

### Organization and prioritization

Difficulty getting or staying organized, or prioritizing tasks.

- Color-coded system for files, projects, or activities
- Color-coding scheme to prioritize tasks
- Weekly chart to identify daily work activities
- Job coach to teach/reinforce organization skills
- Mentor to help the employee
- Supervisor assigns task prioritization
- Electronic organizers, mobile devices, and email reminders
- Assign a new project only when the previous one is complete, when possible
- "Cheat sheet" of high-priority activities, projects, people
- Organize the workspace to reduce clutter
- Separate work areas with complete sets of supplies for differing tasks
- Scheduled weekly time to clean/organize the workspace
- End-of-day routine to organize and set up for the next day

### Multi-tasking

Difficulty performing many tasks at once, regardless of similarity or frequency.

- Separate tasks so each can be completed one at a time
- Flow-chart of tasks that must be done at the same time, labeled or color-coded
  in sequential or preferential order
- Individualized training for multi-tasking techniques (e.g., typing while on
  the phone)
- Identify which tasks must be simultaneous and which can be individual
- Specific feedback to target areas of improvement
- Remove or reduce distractions from the work area
- Ergonomic equipment to facilitate multi-tasking
- Clearly stated performance standards (completion time, accuracy rates)

### Paperwork

Difficulty completing paperwork efficiently — partly from distractions, time
management, disorganization, or prioritization problems.

- Automate paperwork with electronic files where possible
- Speech recognition software to enter text or data
- Pre-filled forms, completing information in advance, or pre-printed stickers
- Checklists in place of writing text
- Templates of letters or emails
- Color-code forms for easy identification
- Re-design commonly used forms: large font, double/triple spacing, adequate
  space for handwritten responses

### Social skills

Difficulty with appropriate social skills: interrupting, poor listening, ineffective
communication.

- Job coach to help understand social cues
- Identify areas of improvement in a fair, consistent manner
- Minimize personal conversation in work areas (or move it away from work areas)
- Sensitivity/disability-awareness training for all employees
- Model appropriate social skills
- Adjust the supervisory method to fit the employee's needs
- Adjust the method of communication to suit the employee
- Work from home

### Attendance

Difficulty getting to work promptly because of varied activities, processes, and
interruptions while preparing to leave home or during the commute.

- Flexible work environment: flexible scheduling; modified break schedule;
  work from home/flexi-place

### Getting to work on time

(Employers may hold time and attendance standards for everyone. These ideas are for
employees whose lateness stems from executive function deficits.)

- Routine of putting and keeping things in their place (keys, phone, glasses)
- Prepare for the next day's work the night before
- Checklist for self and others
- Sticky notes on the door, dashboard, or wherever they will be seen
- Turn off distractions, including cell phones
- Timer or programmable watch to pace the morning routine
